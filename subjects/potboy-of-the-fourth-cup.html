Potboy of the Fourth Cup

Potboy at the Fourth Cup · the one who laughed · unnamed · 13 on his sheet

The Fourth Cup is where the Wine Gate talks, loudly, inaccurately, and with a great deal of confidence, and in the first three decades of the Concordat, by the manuscript’s count, eleven thousand evenings of it go by without anyone once attributing the droughts of the plain to the thing that had caused them. The manuscript lets the potboy into exactly one. On the anniversary of the second Portion a stout, damp, philosophical carter named Bakar says over his fourth cup, with the finality of a man who has been listening to theology all evening, “I do not care whose rain it is, so long as it falls on a road I have already paid for.” It is the most intelligent remark ever made in the Fourth Cup, and nobody remembers it by morning. The potboy laughed, and Medea, pouring the Unclaimed Cup at the empty end of the table, did not.

That is the whole of him: a laugh, and the girl who does not join it. The manuscript gives him no name, age, face or voice, and says nothing of what became of him. Medea, who pours the cup, is the other figure in the sentence.

The sheet’s age of 13, gangly build of about 5 ft 1 in, freckled olive skin, tousled sandy-brown hair, green eyes, chipped tooth, grey tunic, wine-red apron and tray of cups are its own, as are its panels Laughing, Grinning, Looking Aside and Surprised. Its scene, Laughs at the carter’s remark that Medea does not laugh at, follows the manuscript. The line it prints for him, Ha! Medea does not laugh at?, is not in the manuscript.

“The potboy laughed, and Medea, pouring the Unclaimed Cup at the empty end of the table, did not.”
