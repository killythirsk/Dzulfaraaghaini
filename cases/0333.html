  • At dusk at the foot of the Great Beacon in Torrelume, on the evening the Ring of Val Muta is closed, Higaide, who has been told in the Lamp-Hall that a man out of a stone would be entered as unaccounted for, walks out to where the tower’s iron foot goes into the water. He puts his left palm flat against the cold iron, bows to it and holds the bow for ten slow breaths, while the Lamp-Host drill on the quay.

Bortolo Zanni, the ferry-master, stands in the stern of the lead boat with one hand on the sweep; Nico and Ilario ride with the flame, and the islanders, who have never been visited at the flame by anything but the flame, stare upward.

  • On the ninth evening of the river, the first thing the settlers see of Val Muta is a graveyard: a low fence of split stakes, sixty-odd mounds, each marked with a flat river-stone, and at the far end a shovel planted upright in the ground. Ubaldo beside it lifts his hand, slowly, and waves. The goats come off the boats in a clattering, aggrieved flood, the boatmen carry the children ashore, and Ottone, at the foot of the first gangplank, writes each family in a single line apiece.

  • On the third dusk in Val Muta the Ring is closed in what the settlers call simply the lighting. Gaudenzio says the three verses over the Hearth-Lamp and touches his taper to the flame, and the lamplighters carry the fire round the line in relays, a necklace of warm points assembling itself out of the dark. Ilario, raised to lamplighter that afternoon with a handshake and a small brass badge, runs the final eleven in a flat-out terror of dropping something.

  • On the twenty-first of Iverin, a little after the tenth hour, Ilario, nineteen, sits on the low stool beside the iron post of Lamp Forty-One, the Far Lamp, which he has taken for the night from Nico, and a warm, pale, many-lipped flower opens at his feet. He bends toward it as one bends toward a fire; the moths rise off the lamp and settle on its rim, and it takes thirty-one seconds by the count of the sexton, who watches from his fence and does not lift the shovel.

  • On the day of the Great Lighting, after five days of stacking, two hundred men of the parish cross the Line at noon under the Legate’s banner, roll four hundred barrels of the Order’s blessed oil to the rim of the hollow and empty them over the faggots stacked against the stalks. Gaudenzio speaks the three verses over the rim; at the last word two hundred torches go down, and it burns for eleven hours, white at first, with a roar that the whole valley felt in its chest.
